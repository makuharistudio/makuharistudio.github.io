This book chronicles the biographee's life from a childhood taken as political hostage by the Oda then the Imagawa clan at Sunpu (Shizuoka). Then through a military and diplomatic journey forging him into the Third Great Unifier of Japan, bringing around 260 years of national peace to a country that had been fragmented by war for 400 years prior.

My interest in Tokugawa Ieyasu stemmed from the recent Shogun television series, and wanting to know more about the real-life person it was based on. I saw parallels in how his political cunning was portrayed compared to Lee Kuan Yew, father of Singapore, and wanted to see how deep those parallels ran. I also discovered how much more intricate and convoluted the real story is.

I summarised key events and listed lessons in areas including personal integrity, diplomacy, warfare, governance, and a smaller section comparing facts to the fictional television series.



## Context

Please note Japanese names are in family name - given name order.

### Key events

* **December 1542 Birth of Matsudaira Takechiyo**: was born as son to Matsudaira Hirotada with the daughter of a Mikawa lord (p.4).

* **1548 Taken hostage by Oda Nobuhide**: Takechiyo is intended to be taken as hostage of Imagawa Yoshimoto, but was seized by Toda Yasumitsu and Toda Goro, and sent to Oda Nobuhide, staying at Nagoya for 3 years (p.6).

* **1549 Taken hostage by Imagawa Yoshimoto**: Following the death of Matsudaira Hirotada, Oda Nobuhiro surrenders Takechiyo to Sessai to attend his father's funeral in Okazaki, then is sent as hostage to Sunpu, Imagawa's capital (p.8). Imagawa men treat Mikawa men resentfully (p.14).

* **1557 Renamed Matsudaira Jirosaburo Motonobu by Imagawa Yoshimoto**: (p.11).

* **1557 Renamed Matsudaira Kurando Motoyasu**: in honour of his grandfather Matsudaira Kiyoyasu (p.14).

* **1558 Led his first battle**: Under orders of Imagawa Yoshimoto against Suzuki Shigeteru's forces at Terabe castle, who switched alliances from Imagawa Yoshimoto to Oda Nobunaga. Motoyasu successfully repelled Oda Nobunaga's reinforcements (p.15).

* **1560 Imagawa Yoshimoto killed by Oda Nobunaga's forces**: In the battle of Oke-hazama, raising Oda Nobunaga as one of Japan's foremost military leaders (p.19)

* **1560 Matsudaira Motoyasu moves into Okazaki castle**: Following withdrawal of Imagawa officers. Okazaki is the ancestral home of Matsudaira Motoyasu's clan and Mikawa men. Quickly expanded his territory into Owari, stopping when he encountered Oda Gemba in Kutsukake (p.19)

* **1561 Matsudaira Motoyasu allies with Oda Nobunaga**: Turning against less competent Imagawa Ujizane. 

* **1562 Renamed Matsudaira Ieyasu**: After learning from a priest that _"...the greatness of the Minamoto family began with Yoshiie"_ (p.32).

* **1569 Renamed Tokugawa Ieyasu**: After petitioning the Emperor to resume the surname originally borne by Minamoto Yoshisue to reaffirm his connection to the time-honoured line and stand out from other Matsudaira families (p.36).

* **1582 Akechi Mitsuhide kills Oda Nobunaga**: As revenge for disrespecting the dinner he prepared for Oda Nobunaga and Tokugawa Ieyasu by setting Honnou-ji on fire with Oda Nobunaga and his son inside, forcing them to commit seppuku (p.71-72).

* **1583 Tokugawa Ieyasu allies with Oda Nobuo**: Against Toyotomi Hideyoshi, his former colleague who also allied and served under Oda Nobunaga previously (p.76).

* **1584 Toyotomi Hideyoshi reaches an agreement with Oda Nobuo**: Taking away Tokugawa Ieyasu's pretext to oppose him, forcing an alliance (p.88).

* **1590 Anti-Houjou campaign to take eastern Japan**: Toyotomi Hideyoshi rewards Tokugawa Ieyasu with the whole Kwanto region, the latter making Edo his capital (p.108-113).

* **1591 Toyotomi Hideyoshi becomes Taiko**: Passing the office of Kambaku to his nephew Hidetsugu, his adopted heir (p.128).

* **1592-1598 Toyotomi Hideyoshi's Korean campaign**: Fruitless until his death, weakening his feudal lords of resources, but left Tokugawa Ieyasu strengthened as he stayed in Japan (p.128).

* **1600 Will Adams arrives in Japan, and the Sekigahara campaign**: Adams meets Tokugawa Ieyasu at Osaka castle, then during the latter's return to Edo, Ishida Mitsunari declares war. Tokugawa defends control over the two roads to the capital, the Nakasendo and the Tokaido, leading to victory and Ishida's execution in Kyoto (p.138-141, 161).

* **1603 Tokugawa Ieyasu is appointed Shogun**: (p.170).

* **1604-1614 Tokugawa Ieyasu orders samurai lords (daimyo) to help rebuild Edo**: Several other castles were reconstructed from the daimyo by supplying labour and material relative to their respective incomes (p.170, 174).

* **1605 Tokugawa Ieyasu retires from the office of Shogun**: Passing it to his son Tokugawa Hidetada (p.213).

* **1609 commercial relations begin between Holland and Japan**: Japan closes itself off from all other nations (p.180).

* **1611 commercial trade with the Portuguese resets**: Following the sinking of Portuguese ship Madre de Dios in Nagasaki, as retaliation for Japanese sailors being killed in a Macao port (p.194-196).

* **1614 Tokugawa Ieyasu orders all Jesuits to leave Japan, and Osaka winter campaign**: Following accounts from Will Adams and Nishi Soshin regarding Spaniards and Christianity, and a report of Christian-converted Okubo Nagayasu's plans to overthrow Tokugawa (p.188-190). Tokugawa removes the incompatible Christianity from Japan. Tokugawa then ruptures relations with Osaka castle led by the son of the previous Taiko, Toyotomi Hideyori, arranging a peace treaty that significantly weakens the castle's fortifications (p.219-226).

* **1615 Osaka summer campaign**: The peace treaty was expectedly broken by Toyotomi Hideyori's forces, leading to the fall of Osaka castle against Tokugawa Ieyasu and his allies from throughout Japan (p.227-234). The Tokugawa Shogunate removed its last opposition.

* **1616 Tokugawa Ieyasu passes away**: His body first laid at Kunouzan mountain (p.260).

* **1617 Tokugawa Ieyasu is deified as Tosho Daigongen by the Emperor**: His body is moved as part of his will, to Nikko (p.260).



## BOOK NOTES

### DEFINE YOUR GOALS

If you have two absolute objectives, one that visibly achieves a social good, and the other a correlating elevation of your station, frame them carefully.

Framing the station elevation goal at all can be used against you. Begin by publicly framing your social good objective as both your means and end. Do not explicitly engage your station elevation goal until the time is right to claim it, or at least do so subtly and gradually.

Given the biographee's admiration for his Minamoto ancestry, he likely intended to become Shogun in addition to wanting peace throughout Japan, though the author claims no signs were shown of the former. When other daimyo suggested him to pursue it, his reply was:

_"The country has to be set in order, and the welfare of the people seen to. That is the most urgent need ...When all that has been done it will be time to look to my own personal status"_ (p.169).

### OBSERVE A CULTURE OF RESPECT

* **Respect your elders**: Even when trying to perform an act of respect, do not use it as an excuse to impose on your elders. The young should give way to elders (p.13).

* **Respect your peers**: This may even take precedence over a sense of thrift. The biographee had expensive paper for writing to daimyos and other high ranked people, and once stopped a follower from taking a sheet to wrap a piece of ginseng, explaining the paper must never be used for ordinary purposes as to uphold his respect and sincerity for his peers (p.295).

* **Respect your enemies**: If you have a more powerful ally, the kind who mocks corpses of defeated enemies, do not stoop to their level. Show the proper respect, and in a manner that does not offend that ally either, such as by calmly reasoning out loud why the enemy lost. The people will remember you for your better character (p.70).

* **Avoid needless gossip**: Because it involves disrespecting others, and thus can get you killed (p.54).

* **Go against the cultural grain to favour a logical approach**: 3 chief retainers of the biographee's deceased son committed suicide. This displeased the biographee who typically revered ancestral rules, but argued this was a bad one. So he prohibited that custom to respect the loyalty's intention to also serve heirs (p.271).

### PERSONAL INTEGRITY

* **Live for the greater good**: Become reputed for a lifestyle that is clearly not self-serving by being respectful, thrifty, and helpful. From there, you will be positioned to persuade others your interests are for the greater good (p.297).

* **Live thriftily**: Integrity in social administration is measured by how well extravagance is removed from society to improve public civility, mindfulness, and prevent disorder (p.281). An empire must be managed economically (p.292), and thrift is the antithesis to thoughtless extravagance. Example approaches:

  - If your people are not living or sleeping peacefully, then you ought to avoid luxuries too, by eating cheaper and simpler meals (p.30).
  - Do not allow government officials to overdress for their station, as it mounts taxes, leaves lower classes embarrassed (p.292), may be gaudy for the countryside, and may antagonise family traditions (p.102).
  - If something breaks, do not over-emphasise what its replacement is made out of or looks like, as long as it serves the purpose (p.228).
  - Do not provide luxuries for your horses such as winter quilts, as it robs them the chance to become resistant to the cold (p.281).
  - Tell the palace cook to keep the pickled radishes salty so the ladies-in-waiting eat less (p.299).
  - If your pages practice wrestling, reverse the floor mats so they do not damage the good side (p.299).
  - Wash your undergarments rather than wasting money buying new ones (p.291).
  - Do not use elaborate commissariat arrangements. Something comparable to the living standards of a very small retainer is sufficient (p.153).

  Every amount saved, no matter how meagre compared to total resources, must be kept for military costs, and for when the Empire needs it in the future (p.30, 291).

* **Do not indulge in the frivolous**: But do not mock it either, as it is something others value.
  - The biographee did not care for useless amusements, but saw the political value in attending extravagant parties hosted by others, and Noh performances (p.130, 277).
  - The biographee emphasised to his retainers the priority in paying them rather than indulging in luxuries, when he received a gift of peaches from Oda Nobunaga that initially impressed them (p.30).
  
* **Fight honourably**: Even if it forgoes short-term opportunities. During the anti-Houjou campaign, the biographee was allied to the militarily superior Toyotomi Hideyoshi. The biographee's retainers recommended assassinating Toyotomi during his camp visits as his personal guard was sparse, but the biographee argued he could not betray anyone that way (p.109). His betrayal methodology having standards doubly served as a survival strategy by having his enemies think of him a negotiable man. Remember that real men shoot at enemies that march up to them (p.45).

* **Hope for the best from your enemies**: But be prepared for the worst. Make public displays of trust for your enemy, and let them self-implode from their betrayal to retain your moral ground. During the Osaka winter campaign, the biographee arranged as part of the enemy's surrender, that their castle's outer walls and moats be removed. Instead of finishing them off, the biographee waited until Osaka castle openly took to arms, morally justifying the biographee's final attack on it (p.227-228).

* **Display an effort toward personal integrity**: At least as far as your goals can afford. Like denying rewards that your lineage is known to have previously exploited (p.196-197). The moral ground of saying you do not wish to follow the same path may also be used as submissive cover.

* **Test the integrity of your successors**: The legacy of a high integrity life can be stained by a successor. The biographee sent his son a _"young flower"_ bearing cakes out of concern for his son's loneliness. The flowery envoy reported back only of the successful delivery of the cakes, as nothing else happened (p.293).

### KEEP YOUR COMPOSURE 

* **Be patient and have self-control**: As it invites fewer failures (p.21). One of the biographee's maxims is: _"Patience is the foundation of security and long life: consider anger as an enemy"_. Decisions made whilst upset can be severely self-injurious. So it also helps to have someone rein you in when you are about to do something objectively unwise (p.231), if you are lacking in patience. Having patience means constantly practicing the effort of not giving way to the emotions of _"...joy, anger, anxiety, love, grief, fear, and hate"_ (p.317).

* **Refrain from an openly bitter disposition**: Otherwise the victim of your bitterness might come back to bite you (p.76).

### HAVE HUMILITY

* **Blame yourself, not others**: One of the biographee's key maxims. Have the humility to recognise your shortcomings, the ability to learn from others, then overcome reality's severity.

  - **Have purposeful humility**: Politics is vicious against genuine weakness. The biographee had the humility to act like he was deceived, when he was actually deceiving others. Be willing to play the fool when the stakes do not matter, but be sharp when they do (p.297-298).

  - **Make humility a shared effort**: The biographee met an embarrassed retainer who had been farming and muddied his face hoping the biographee would not recognise him. The biographee still did and told the retainer to go with the times, and that all must learn unpleasant tasks, regardless how pathetic it felt (p.13).

  - **Do not exaggerate your status**: Do not try to compete with an ally on something unimportant, like uniqueness of entertainment, if you suspect they have misgivings about you, as that will just fuel their uncertainty. It is wiser to appear ordinary and honest (p.299).

### SEEK SELF-IMPROVEMENT

* **Find ideals to strive for**: The biographee insisted that the art of the warrior comes first from imitating minister I Yin and councilor Lu Chang, men famous for their character, wisdom, and leadership, more so than weapon proficiency (p.317).

* **Everyone is different, so train to individual needs**: When trying to train a retainer to be loyal, do so by separating them from other youths or based on their personality. Do not use a fixed training plan. By educating them with benevolence, they in turn will be more inclined to love their duty (p.318).
  
### BE BENEVOLENT

* **Kindness can pay dividends**: By carefully choosing humaneness over hostility. Oda Nobunaga tried to exterminate Takeda Katsuyori's remaining samurai. Some fled into the biographee's territory where they were kindly treated. In return, several hundred joined the biographee to claim the previous Takeda lands after Oda Nobunaga's death (p.73).

* **Be visibly generous to your retainers**: But do not sell yourself completely short either, by assessing the perceived versus actual value of rewards over time and investment. Following victory in the anti-Houjou campaign, the biographee gifted his retainers the most thriving towns, then made a dilapidated 130 year old castle with a poor village named Edo his capital (p.117). This seems like buying loyalty, but that is short-term thinking. He likely assessed that in the long-term, his higher standing and revenues could build Edo into greatness.

### RATIONALITY AND REASONABLENESS

* **Pursue rationality and reason**: The author describes the biographee as strongly rationalist and nationalist, sometimes purely instructive and rather conservative, with shrewd insights into human nature, using rational plans to leverage those insights (p.313).

* **Protect allies with different views**: A key strength of rationality is being able to impartially listen to different points of view, then reason the most informed way forward. That requires protecting councilors with opposing views from killing each other. Do this by explaining the fundamental nature of those councilors, e.g. outlining that someone may simply be ultraconservative in their views (p.20) which is fine, because it is explicitly known and can be fairly accounted for in the reasoning process.

* **Solicit genuine information from others**: The absence of different views can lead to poor decision-making. Thus if engaged with someone unwilling to divulge what they really think, press hard and ask for it at least once (p.289), otherwise you risk making uninformed decisions.

### COMMUNICATION TIP

* **Be considerate by being concise**: Do not pad out information with fluff, as it inconsiderately prevents others from taking corrective actions sooner. Write orders concisely so none can claim ignorance (p.33).

### BE EVER VIGILANT

* **Vigilance as the antithesis to corruption**: Corruption is the open wound that bleeds out the effort of a country's people, so take ongoing measures to prevent it. Keep a constant eye out for any impulsive movements of your cash (p.13).

* **Keep constant political surveillance**: As a region's lord, you are responsible for keeping an eye on entire borders, and keeping informed on foreign country activities (p.284).

### BUILD FORESIGHT

* **Prevention is better than cure**: Use analytical foresight to predict what could come and make preparations to defend against or leverage it. The biographee constantly tried to anticipate every possible contingency (p.259), like how proper construction can escape earthquake danger, or having a daimyo family's clan living separately so a single lightning strike does not destroy the entire clan (p.290-291).

### READ FOR PRACTICALITY

* **Extensively read, then put into practice**: The ruler of the Empire must know how to learn. As books were the only source of knowledge, the biographee emphasised their publication as the first principle of good government. He said that the ruler should know the Four Books of Confucius or at least the 4th book, Mencius (p.249, 252). He was also a bibliophile preferring practical texts to improve his intellect or function in ruling the Empire, and he did not care about emotional works (p.244, 247).

### PUBLIC IMAGE

* **Become a defender of national treasures**: A national leader's public image is tied to the nation's image, which includes its treasures. If any of them are damaged or destroyed, pursue their restoration, otherwise you will be remembered as a lord who would not restore them (p.30-31).

### DECISION-MAKING

* **Do not let your emotions cloud your judgment**: As it could cost you your life. The biographee had a carbuncle growing on him with odds against his survival. Out of pride or fear, the he refused his retainer's plea to see a doctor to treat it. They argued until the retainer threatened to commit suicide, as it was better than a destitute life as a masterless and elderly beggar. The biographee was convinced by the rationale and successfully treated (p.103-104).

* **Find and resolve the real source of problems**: The biographee was furious at his son's delay in joining the Sekigahara campaign, due to over-ambitiousness in taking a castle en route. He asked if his son's staff did not advise the futility of its capture as it would be surrendered upon the campaign's victory. His son confessed that Toda Issai did so, who was then summoned to the biographee. He said that Toda's low position led to Toda's advice not being followed. So he awarded Toda a castle and 10 times his current income, positioning him to be listened to in future (p.150).

* **Strike while the iron is hot**: Move swiftly on your decisions. You need to be exceptionally rapid at collecting your forces' strength (p.117) before enemies take advantage of your post-war recovery.

* **Do not take non-profitable risks**: Ensure your decisions support critical efforts and do not jeopardise them via useless risks (p.280-281).

### NEGOTIATION

* **Do not feel unnecessarily indebted**: Even if you receive gifts from someone in the context of something like congratulations, it does not obligate you to them (p.75).

* **Keep track of allowances**: So you can leverage them in future endeavours. If a more powerful ally lets you off easy on deliverables due to travel distance, make a note of it so you can be remiss in future with little question (p.131).

### LEADERSHIP

* **Leadership spearheads culture**: The biographee describes culture as having a logical outlook that investigates and navigates affairs without mistakes, and passing judgment, punshment, and rewards fearlessly and without bias, whilst still being sympathetic (p.314).

* **The conquering commander**:
  - Does not sit behind gazing at his men's backs.
  - Charges with the greatest vigour and boldness.
  - Understands that victory depends on fortune.
  - Prudently knows that too much discretion invites loss (p.279).

* **The military art of the general**:
  - Is wholly devoted to the technical side of war.
  - Is ever vigilant in keeping men trained, intellectual, spirited, and in stamping out disorder (p.314).

* **Duties of a ruler**:
  - Set aside your arrogance, pursue a policy of compassion, and prepare for rebellion and disorder, to maintain the Empire's peace and security.
  - Work for the prosperity of your descendants, pursue integrity in conduct, discern the good and bad points of your councillors, and administer the people's affairs without fear or favour (p.285, 314).

* **Appoint genuine leaders, not figureheads**: A figurehead is a precursor to inevitable defeat. In the biographee's campaign against Osaka castle, its defenders lacked good leadership, with Toyotomi Hideyori deflatedly failing to rouse enthusiasm among officers he rarely visited, and Lady Yodo with her ladies-in-waiting inspecting posts but leaving the soldiers with mixed feelings (p.219-220).

### DIPLOMACY

* **Gather strength, for might makes right**: Being rational and reasonable makes diplomacy easier, and it allows for gains with lower cost and effort. But such diplomacy is useless without force to back it (p.21). Without strength and power, no stable government or even peace is possible, as these depend on armies and money to equip them (p.297).

* **Become a master of words**: Diplomacy involves convincing people to move in ways that benefit the nation. When Emperor Go-Yozei wanted his second son to succeed the throne, his sponsors backed his first son instead. To prevent these sponsors from having their way with the throne, the biographee diplomatically gave his opinion to the Emperor that he should make the decision entirely himself without anyone else's consultation, on the basis that a father knows their son better than anyone (p.169).

* **Be diplomatic, but not a brown-noser**: Being diplomatic can raise suspicions, so you also need cunning to be effective. Toyotomi Hideyoshi once tested the biographee by having Hosokawa Tadaoki cite Hideyoshi's self-boast that he had never been beaten. The biographee contradicted this which reassured Hideyoshi a little (p.296), in that the biographee was being sincere instead of a brown-noser, the latter a quality seen in traitors.

* **National defense depends on national spirit**: Foster a conviction for military spirit among the people, because foreign countries will attack when your martial qualities are relaxed, and conversely you will be more inclined to attack when foreign countries are relaxed (p.284).

* **Use your foreign relations to expand your connections**: If you only have one connection in a region, especially if it is a strong one, ask their permission for your trading with other countries to extend your trade in the region (p.181).

* **Seek unbiased perspectives on unfamiliar tactics**: By never relying on a single source of information. The biographee asked his England-born retainer Will Adams, why the Spanish sought to survey Japan's coasts, and Adams replied it was an act of European hostility, which was also  corroborated by the Portuguese and Spanish exposing each other (p.189).

* **Be uniformly courteous in foreign relations**: But enact punishment when the nation is insulted, otherwise the country invites further assault (p.195).

### MANAGE ALLIANCES

* **Have a close understanding of your allies**: As ignorance is a guise for betrayal. Be insightful to human nature and only start a campaign once you have a grasp of how your generals and retainers think, because victory depends on loyalty (p.160).

* **Have an eye for talented allies**: The biographee's attendants were surprised he remembered a mere rear-vassal's name. This was because of the vassal's impressive insight. During the Sekigahara campaign, the biographee asked Keya Mondo for enemy numbers, Keya estimating 20 to 30 thousand men. The biographee questioned this as others estimated 100 thousand. Keya replied that his estimate was the real number, accounting for the secret traitors among the enemy's forces (p.154).

* **Keep forging alliances**: Do so strategically to protect yourself from potential enemies or to subvert that "ally" (p.77-78, 94).

* **Make others indebted to you**: To coax potential alliances or sow division.
  - **By giving useful advice**: Toyotomi Hideyoshi was angered by a rumoured uprising by Date Masamune and demanded his relocation. Date sent envoys to the biographee requesting a plan to engage Toyotomi's messengers. This involved showing Date's men were viciously against leaving their lands and the biographee himself discussing with Toyotomi their natural feelings. Toyotomi changed his mind and Date was indebted to the biographee (p.124-125).
  - **By taking credit for rewards**: Toyotomi asked the biographee to double the reward he intended for his retainers, to make them feel indebted to Toyotomi, though the biographee just mentioned half of what was intended (p.116, 198).

* **Show sincerity toward allies**: By making an open show of your trust in them,
  - **To build long-term foundations for working together**: When the biographee acquired Kai province, he appointed existing Kai advisors and retainers, especially those most loyal to their previous lord and re-employed them. The Kai people realised the biographee's benevolence and became docile and submissive (p.75).
  - **As a disguise for intended betrayal later**: When the biographee allied himself with the Houjou clan, he insisted on being lower ranked than his Houjou son-in-law, and showed a senior Houjou councilor the dismantling of a castle between their lands to prove sincerity (p.97).

* **Subtly test ally loyalty**: By offering an **out** option to mitigate potential traitors or to coax existing ones into the light (p.141). Tokugawa Hidetada said that after his father's death, he would test daimyo loyalty by sending them home for at least a year, then call them back to Edo, destroying those who refused the command (p.259).

* **Discontinue an alliance**: Respect your councillors by discussing alliance changes with them, even if you plan on proceeding regardless. It lets you be aware of potential traitors. You can also disguise your contacting of an enemy intended to be a later ally, by claiming it a gesture to buy time and protect the current alliance (p.20-21).

### POLITICAL STRATEGY & GOVERNANCE

* **Understand political stakeholder roles and values**: Make it clear whether or not the Imperial Court is subject to military administration. Understand that military nobles are more useful than Court nobles, despite the latter having perceptions of being valuable (p.305).

* **Take measures against the self-destructive potential of political power**: When the people treat tax collection officials with too much respect, it can get to officials' heads to the point they misuse the tax money, and fall into a spiral trying to pay the government back (p.287).

* **Keep the peasant class' needs moderately met**: The biographee's policy was to diet the peasant class carefully, because if they were overfed, they were too gorged to hunt, and if not given enough they became too weak to hunt (p.287).

* **Keep your political enemies (and allies) drained of funds**: To remove their capacity for war (p.206). One approach is to make them bankrupt by trapping them into non-profitable ventures, to help ensure the Empire's peace (p.215).

* **Control the political landscape**:
  - **By making subtle preparations**: Before you trigger relational breakdowns. For example, stocking up on guns and ammunition before fracturing relations to the point they are needed (p.218).
  - **By using rumours to your advantage**: Trick the enemy into openly preparing for war to justify your own attack (p.218).

* **Create consistent and sharp rules to prevent internal disorder**: The biographee had a 13 article legislation for his daimyo that enforced writing and military training for daimyo house members, fief expulsion of any lawbreakers and daimyo who fell into excesses, and measures to prevent cross-fief relations and castle repairs (p.302-303). He also had rules that forced clan families to take responsibility through loss of family office and collective suicide for having uncouth members, and a fief rotation system to mitigate long-term lord oppression over subjects (p.315-316).

### RELIGIOUS POSITION

Be superstitious to the practicality of the superstition or observance, so long as it contributes good order to the land (p.121).

Japanese historian Tokutomi Sohou remarks that the biographee showed interest in religion more than any other Japanese military leader, though as a government instrument (p.309), since that was one of its ancient functions. However, that conflicts with the actual governmental structure, and diverse religious presence inherently arouses conflict, risking a nation's peace. Hence religion must be carefully managed. To address this, the biographee made religious conflict against the law (p.311).

The biographee also indirectly admonished those who did not live according to their espoused faith. He found 10 of Kinryuzan temple's 36 priests to be immaculate, so he invited those 10 to his castle to read sutras. The rest took the hint and left, restoring the temple's atmosphere (p.121).

### WARFARE RULES

* **Give a clear set of field orders for all troops**: To avoid disorder leveragable by enemy forces. The most glaring contradiction in the book is that the biographee set out extensive field orders for the anti-Houjou campaign. These included punishments for those breaking formation, various causes of disorder, or intimidating tradespeople (p.113). When his son as the new Shogun set out regulations for the Osaka campaign using an arguably more summarised version (p.220-221), he criticised his son claiming he never made such detailed orders in his youth, and should leave the details to the circumstances (p.221).

* **Be brief with battle communication**: Adhere to clear, non-nuanced communications during tense situations (p.155).

* **Communicate critical plans to its executors only**: Especially if you do not trust your allies. At a council of Oda Nobunaga's generals, Sakai Tadatsugu requested to attack a troubling part of the enemy force's rear. Oda berated him and Sakai was crestfallen. Oda later sent for Sakai privately, approving Sakai's plan in secret from the council. Nobunaga also provided Sakai with 500 additional reinforcement matchlockmen, as proof of his approval (p.63).

* **Only withdraw upon trusted confirmation**: If an ally recommends retreat upon the reported death of another ally leader, send a runner to verify. Retreating prematurely may jeopardise the campaign and future alliances (p.19).

### MILITARY STRATEGY

* **Set up effective contributors for a military campaign**: Tokutomi Sohou is cited for key reasons in the defeat of Ishida Mitsunari's forces at Sekigahara. Those reasons are inverted here as reasons for successful campaigns.
  - Have a genuine Commander-in-Chief, rather than a figurehead.
  - Have an incapable military commander as an enemy.
  - Have army cohesion, to help coordination and maintain battle morale.
  - Have a resilient fighting spirit, built on strong and clear reasons to fight.
  - Pursue co-operation to help build valour.
  - Be led by someone whose presence disarms enemy morale (making enemies feel like they are on the wrong side), whilst having an enemy commander who is so detested it boosts your own army's morale (making allies know they are in the right) (p.157).

* **Do not underestimate your enemy**: Even if they are small in position or stature. When there is sufficient unrest, a good enough person can rise to leadership despite no such intentions. Such men can gain many followers who admire them and the danger (p.260).
  
* **Follow through with postwar goals**: Even if the battle appears over, do not stop moving until all is in proper order. After his victory at Sekigahara, the biographee did not let his men sing a customary victory song until they freed his allies' hostaged families in Osaka (p.158).

* **Handle remnant forces and dissidents with care**: You can never fully purge an enemy, as their perspectives can persist in many different ways. A long term strategy of amicability may placate the defeated and their sympathisers (p.161).
  - **But deal with the incorrigibly violent as needed**: Following victory at the Osaka campaign, the biographee sought to get rid of the masterless samurai (ronin) that sided with Toyotomi Hideyori, as they were a threat to the Empire's peace (p.233).

* **Use reward systems to further your strategy**: Peace time is preparation time for the next war, and it happens as early as the distribution of rewards after a war.
  - **Reward retainers with fiefs and family with responsibilities**: Retainers need the fiefs to build armies that serve you, and refusing to endow family with fiefs prevents disputes from tearing an alliance apart. Reward family with important strategic positions and administrative control, but not wealth (p.168).
  - **Restructure to defang or keep busy your potentially dangerous allies**: After the Houjou campaign, Toyotomi Hideyoshi asked the biographee to relocate to the Kwanto region, separating the biographee from his loyalists (p.116).
  - **Reward your most trusted retainers into strategic positions**: After the biographee vacated his ancestral domain, Toyotomi Hideyoshi positioned his retainers such they bordered off the Kwanto region (p.117).

### WARFARE TACTICS

* **Surround an enemy to cut off their communications and wear down their supplies**: In the Osaka winter campaign, the biographee said taking over the outer castle followed by a waiting policy could be used to hem in the inner castle and cut off their communications (p.221).

* **Use diplomacy as a dual weapon**: The biographee had an approach of _"...gripping the throat and stroking the back"_, which involved negotiating with or bribing the enemy whilst attacking them (p.223).
  - The biographee enticed enemies Ono Harunga and Hayami Kai-ko-kami to his camp to discuss peace terms, and though the talk itself failed, their return to Osaka instilled doubt in the spirits of their men. Conversely, if they had joined the biographee's side, that would have cheered his own men (p.229).

* **Send false information to home cities to alleviate war concerns**: Otherwise home city anxieties could lead to morale loss on the battlefield. Like sending the head of some random warrior back home, claiming it to be the enemy leader's head (p.47).

* **Exploit the environment for attacks**: Take advantage of rainy weather that concentrates enemy forces together. Create the illusion your forces are in one position as a distraction (e.g. camping opposite with dummy troops), then lead a sudden charge to scatter the concentrated enemy into confusion, and take out their leader. Used by Oda Nobunaga against Imagawa Yoshimoto at the battle of Oke-hazama (p.18).

* **Do not cross a river to pursue an enemy**: It could cut you off from ally support. Ishida Mitsunari's strategist, Shima Sakon, attacked Nakamura Kazuuji's men (ally to the biographee), then Shima's forces retreated across Kuisegawa river. Nakamura's men chased those retreating forces but were led into a fatal ambush (p.152-153).

* **Disguise your real attack**: During the Osaka winter campaign, the biographee ordered bombardment across 3 consecutive days at 10pm and dawn, whilst miners undermined the castle towers (p.222).

* **Read into your enemy general's intentions**: Mori Nagayoshi's army faced the biographee's, whose battalion did not move. Mori misread this as his intention to help ally Ii Naomasa's men, so Mori waited for the biographee's army to divide. But the biographee divided his army into 2 and sicced both on Mori, splitting Mori's men until Mori himself was exposed for an Ii matchlock man that headshot Mori (p.84).

* **Force critical leverage with an ultimatum**: If your victory depends on a traitor within enemy ranks, and they aren't moving as agreed, use an ultimatum to scare them into action, before the battle attrition robs your ability to enforce it. Like shooting at them to see their reaction (p.156).

* **Exhaust your enemy by keeping them tense**: Especially in places and at times they think they are safe. The biographee's strategy involved bombarding Osaka castle so that the ladies could not rest, and launching sudden volleys and war cries at random times in the night to keep the enemy on their toes (p.223).

* **Use evasive manoeuvres to exhaust much larger armies**: Large sluggish armies, especially if led by less comparable generals, can be led on wild goose chases by smaller forces (p.74).

* **Camouflage your retreat to scare off pursuers**: The biographee did this when retreating into Hamamatsu castle by ordering the gates to be left open and flares lit to trick pursuers into assuming an ambush and stopping their pursuit (p.47).

* **Never surrender to despair**: Keep operating on the assumption there is a way out of a bad situation, as _"You can never tell how things will turn out the next minute, and so while you have breath in your body you have got to take care of yourself"_ (p.161).

## WARTIME ECONOMY

* **Spend thriftily and go makeshift**: If you observe warriors with heavy armour are more often wounded than warriors who do not, then do not waste money on useless armour. Also, equip your soldiers with multipurpose gear so they carry less, like using light steel hats to cook rice (p.280).

* **Keep your money working for you**: As a retainer, you should spend money given to you by your lord productively, as hoarding it is thievery, and conversely going into debt is foolish (p.159).

### NATION-BUILDING

* **Adhere to ancient rules that still have practical relevance**: The biographee did not change Takeda Shingen's laws when he took over Kai province, other than lightening taxes so the people welcomed him, and adhering to their rules earned their goodwill (p.310).

* **Find experts with ideas to improve the nation**: The first ideas that improved Japan's economy were coin cutting for size convenience and fixing the price of silver to stabilise commodity prices (p.124).

* **Forbid unproductive industries**: Particularly in the early stages of nation-building. The biographee issued an edict forbidding smoking as an indulgence that wasted time and money (p.175), and his grandson forbade growing tobacco on cultivable land (p.326).

* **Manage price stability as an ongoing effort**: The biographee hoarded gold and silver to control their circulation. The less in circulation, the more valuable they became. The more in circulation, the more ashamed people felt if they did not have it. Similarly, if the price of rice rose, he would sell his rice to circulate more and lower its price, and when it was too cheap, he bought more of it (p.294).

* **Apply swift and severe judicial sentencing**: The biographee believed justice should be fast and fierce, not slow to process. This is because people avoid harsh conditions, whereas they are more willing to risk themselves in slower uncertainty (p.309).

### CURRENT JAPAN

There are a few notes that link back to customs in modern day Japan.

* **The Tokugawa Shogunate influenced the self-restrained character of the Japanese people**: Tokutomi Sohou is cited regarding the over-the-top regalia which Toyotomi Hideyoshi wore when riding for the anti-Houjou campaign was such a spectacle that stands were erected along the country. The current Japanese culture of self-restraint and dislike of display and attention-seeking are owed to the Tokugawa Shogunate, as they were not prominent values during Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi's rule (p.108).

* **The Japanese do not concern themselves much with overseas enterprises**: (p.233).

* **Conformism is prioritised over individuality for positions of responsibility**: Official Japanese positions only care about the system, which makes them dangerous for those more individualist leaning (p.256).

* **Regulations are systematically focused on collective well-being**: In 1642, Tokugawa Iemitsu he made it forbidden that land be sold for all time, to prevent farmers wealthy enough to buy more farmland to perpetually grow in wealth and at the expense of the collective (p.327). Though this was abolished 4 years into the Meiji Restoration (Grok).

### NOTABLE PROVERBS

* The biographee considered himself relatively unlearned in his old age due to all the fighting he did in his youth, but from those times he held onto 2 sayings by Lao-tze:
  - _"Those who understand how to be content with their lot are always happy"_
  - _"Recompense enmity with kindness"_ (p.251).

* One of the biographee's maxims is: _"If you want to tell lies that will be believed, don't tell the truth that won't"_ (p.299).

* The biographee once said that the value of a person comes from their natural character, and that those who take on the guise of other people's qualities without inherent abilities of their own, are incompatible as companions for great leaders. This also reflects the low social standing actors in Japan had at that time (p.10).



## TV SHOW COMPARISON

To preface, the comparisons here relate to the 2024 TV series by Rachel Kondo and Justin Marks, starring Sanada Hiroyuki, as I have not read the original source material.

### Similarities

* The fictional "Toranaga Yoshii" when first meeting "John Blackthorne", confiscated the latter's ship and crew, interested in Blackthorne's voyage through the Straits of Magellan (p.139), and later burnt down Blackthorne's ship to keep him around because Toranaga supposedly found him amusing. Their meeting was historically similar, and their continued fictional relationship may yet prove to be the same as how the biographee found Will Adams so useful for shipbuilding and other information, that he would never let Adams return to England (p.179).

* The biographee used his ladies-in-waiting as go betweens and spies for diplomatic purposes. When he was young, he was charged to Acha-no-tsubone, former wife of an Imagawa retainer, who later became one of them and obtained power becoming the Matron of the Ladies-in-Waiting (p.272). Her fictional counterpart "Kiri no Kata" is portrayed as one of "Toranaga's" wives and performs a similar and critical espionage role.

* The fictional show appears to merge two historical wars, the Sekigahara campaign where the biographee fought Ishida Mitsunari's forces, and the Osaka campaign where the biographee sought for the Imperial Court to be subject to military rule. The latter battle critically involved Yodo-dono, the historical counterpart to the fictional "Ochiba no Kata", and both being mothers to the late Taiko's heir. Though their personalities are similar, as the author describes Yodo during the Osaka campaigns as narrow-minded, lacking in real world experience, superstitious, and emotional, thus making her the ideal weak point of Toyotomi Hideyori's force to attack (p.223).

### Differences

* The key difference is that the fictional "Ishido Kazunari" is one of the 5 regents appointed by the Taiko, whereas his real-life counterpart Ishida Mitsunari was not.
  - Historically, Toyotomi Hideyoshi appointed these Five Regents to administer Japan until his son Hideyori was of age to rule:
    1. Tokugawa Ieyasu
    2. Ukita Hideie
    3. Maeda Toshiie
    4. Mori Terumoto
    5. Uesugi Kagekatsu (p.136).
  - And there was a lower ranked set of five senior field administrators (Bugyo):
    1. Ishida Mitsunari
    2. Masuda Nagamori
    3. Maeda Gen-i
    4. Asano Nagamasa
    5. Nagatsuka Masaie (p.135).

* The fictional "Kashigi Yabushige" is portrayed as a schemer who indulges in the questionable side of the period's aesthetics, playing both sides of the war to stay alive. He is supposedly based on the historical Honda Masanobu, a scholarly Mikawa samurai, confidential adviser, and constantly set aside his own interests in favour of his master's, to the point there are no records of him taking advantage of his office and even repeatedly declined the biographee's insistences for promotion (p.253-254). The TV series affirms the historical description, though limited to outcomes, and perhaps not so much in terms of character integrity.

* The author cites Hosokawa Gracia, formerly Akechi Tama, as one of the most advanced ladies of her time, being famously highly intelligent, versed in multiple languages, and beautiful in looks and character (p.148). Whilst her fictional counterpart "Toda Mariko" is similarly portrayed, their fates are very different. The TV series has a strategic attack-purposed end for "Mariko-sama", but the author indicates 2 stories on the historical fate of Gracia, favouring a defiant one where Ogasawara Shosai, retainer of the Hosokawa household, stabbed Gracia with his halberd through her room's door, set fire to the building, then committed seppuku when Ishida Mitsunari's forces arrived (p.149).

* The series showed fictional "Toranaga Yoshii" cleverly fleeing Osaka castle under the control of  "Ishido Kazunari" by hiding in a lady's palanquin. But historically the positions were reversed, for Ishida Mitsunari was the one who escaped in a lady's palanquin from Osaka castle when he heard rumours that seven generals aligned to Tokugawa Ieyasu sought to kill him, with Ishida appealing for Tokugawa's protection until he returned to his home castle in Sawayama (p.136-137).

* The fictional "Tonomoto Akinao" is a samurai posing as fisherman working for "Toranaga Yoshii", the latter ordering the former to convert to Christianity to spy on them. I believe this character is meant to be historically based on a tea master named Nishi Soshin, whom Tokugawa Ieyasu sent to "the West" to investigate Christianity for 3 years (p.189-190).

## DISCUSSION

The long term strategies of both Tokugawa Ieyasu of Japan and Lee Kuan Yew of Singapore depended critically on aligning with more powerful allies whilst maintaining personal integrity such that their side of the alliance would earn the people's support as the lesser of two evils, and making their successive efforts more plausible. This also required them both to be exceptionally patient, shrewd, and appear trustworthy when they were not, ready to become traitorous when the time was right (p.296).

Another similarity is that both leaders had industrious right-hand men that built their nations. Lee Kuan Yew had Goh Keng Swee for Singapore, and the biographee had Ina Kumazo Tadatsugu for Japan. When allied with Toyotomi Hideyoshi and relocated to Edo, Tokugawa made Ina commissioner for the Eight Provinces of eastern Japan. His allies feared this decision, so the biographee had Ina sign an oath to administer them impartially. The biographee offhandedly came up with the oath's rules, such was the trust and confidence he had in Ina, who made a name for himself as an engineer constructing dams, land reclamation, increasing the overall revenue of Kwanto, and creating the Ina and Hikosaka schools of local government (p.120). Perhaps every great leader requires an efficient task machine at their side...

In terms of judging others, both leaders similarly open-mindedly assessed others on the basis of actual merit and possible use for their nations, rather than blindly listening to bad-mouthing, or based on ethnicity. In Lee Kuan Yew's case, his platform was of ethnic equality and personal achievement, even taking in former enemies who found their way back to common sense. The clearest example for the biographee was defying Portuguese accusations that Will Adams was a pirate heretic (p.139) and took him in as a close retainer. In that sense, reasonableness and diplomacy were these leader's strongest weapons, supported by qualities like adaptability and self-control (p.70).

Of lesser note is that both Tokugawa Ieyasu and Lee Kuan Yew were famous for eating very simple and plain meals for their times. Perhaps due to a combination of personal health concerns and frugality, but it does offer insight that both were at least not in their roles for the sake of power alone, as they were willing to forego an easily attainable improvement in pleasure, which is a rudimentary indicator of priority leanings between oneself and the people.

A lot of the lessons I learned reading Lee Kuan Yew's autobiography are mirrored and applied in Tokugawa's own life. Though chronologically speaking, I should say that it is the other way around. Perhaps Tokugawa Ieyasu's enthusiastic reading of ancient Chinese texts happened to overlap with the methodologies potentially learned by Lee Kuan Yew, hence the feeling of deja vu I have in watching Shogun and reading Sadler's book, when linking the two men. Or perhaps this is simply a standard for east Asian leaders, given their similarity implies replicability.

My favourite quote is one that I hope encourages similar people to rise up in the future when they are needed. The author describes the biographee in a way that can also be said of Lee Kuan Yew, in that his efforts and achievements were done with _"...an ordinary personality developed to an extraordinary degree...He did just what most people would do, or expect to see done"_ (p.296).



## REVIEW

A lot occurs and this book tries to account for all the key people, so you may need to forgive (more likely thank) the writer for decidedly skimming some by name. It can get a little difficult to keep track of individuals and their allegiances, but at no fault of the author, so much the culture of the time allowed names to be bestowed or changed such that you can get confused on whom was related to whom. But it is manageable. 

The overarching campaign of the biographee's life is easy to grasp, with interluding short chapters on other notable retainers and individuals of the period. Even though you develop quite a picture of the biographee until his death, I very much enjoyed Chapter 42 entitled _"Ieyasu's personal habits and views"_ which details him even more. It is a compilation of very short anecdotes that the author clearly wanted to keep in the book, but did not integrate into the other chapters to protect the book's overall flow.

My favourite anecdote was when the retired biographee was angry at 2 bugyo for disrupting his hawking by allowing traps to be set in the area. It was amusing to read about the subtle diplomacy even in the relationships within his close circle of himself, his son Hidetada, Acha-no-tsubone, and Honda Masanobu, with the lives of the two bugyo hanging in the balance. It was really hard to tell whom was playing whom in that affair.

This book is an ideal primer for anyone with minor to no knowledge of how the Tokugawa Shogunate came to pass and is looking to delve a little deeper. It covers the biographee's wars leading up to the establishment of the Shogunate, so if you are looking for how it ended with the Meiji Restoration, you would need another source. I myself impulse bought this book when I happened to see its splendid cover art whilst window shopping. Ah, judging a book by its cover, the irony. What I believe to be additional coloured photos inserted for this edition, are also a wonderful treat.

I would also recommend this book to anyone looking to visit Japan that are genuinely interested in visiting historical places of significance, as it mentions quite a few places that are on and off the beaten track that tourists typically take, like Sunpu castle in Shizuoka. Having the knowledge of the journey the biographee took makes it all the more worthwhile.
