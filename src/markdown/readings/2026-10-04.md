This book discusses the practical implications of how humanity could colonise Mars. I summarised key science terms that I was previously unfamiliar with, followed by discussions around the reasons, challenges, social nature, governance, and self-sufficiency raised by the author.

## Science of Martian settlement

The first half of the book illustrates the math, science, and technology available or attainable to facilitate Martian settlement. It explains these well enough that a novice can get through it with some ease, but in my case, I still had to look up a lot of principles at almost every other page for clarity and out of curiosity.

Through Grok, here are some things I learned:

### Physics and space science
* _**bars and millibars**_: 1 bar ≈ air pressure at Earth's 0 elevation. 1 bar = 1000 mb. 6-8 mb ≈ Martian atmosphere.
* _**Earth vs Mars datum for elevation**_: On Earth, 0 elevation is sea level. On Mars, 0 elevation is the planet's mean equatorial radius.
* _**aphelion**_: A point in a planet or object's orbit where it is furthest from the Sun.
* _**Linear No Threshold Method**_: Radiation safety rule-of-thumb that treats every extra dose of radiation as increased cancer risk.
* _**Keplerian elliptical transfer orbit**_: The curved path a spacecraft takes when only under gravity (i.e. when no engines fired). Typically based on Sun's mass e.g. the middle part of the journey from outside Earth's orbit to Mars's orbit.
* _**tokamak**_: A machine triggering fusion by holding a plasma gas hotter than the Sun's core, floated in the shape of a doughnut using a magnetic field.

### Chemistry and material science
* _**basalt**_: Volcanic rock formed from cooled lava. One of the most common rocks on Earth and Mars.
* _**stoichiometric**_: Exact proportions of substances needed for a complete chemical reaction, with nothing left over. The perfect mixture is known as "stoichiometric ratio".
* _**zirconia**_: Hard ceramic material extremely resistant to heat and wear.
* _**dimethyl ether**_: Man-made fuel that is easy to turn into liquid and used like diesel.
* _**Sabatier reaction**_: Chemical process that turns carbon dioxide and hydrogen into methane and water.
* _**Type M asteroids**_: Asteroids that look metallic, based on radar and sunlight reflection.

### Biology
* _**cyanobacteria**_: Contains chlorophyll and splits water to release oxygen. Billions of years ago they pumped oxygen into Earth's air to make the atmosphere.
* _**spirulina algae**_: Edible cyanobacterium rich in protein that is compact and fast-growing.

### Agriculture
* _**double cropping**_: Growing two crops on the same land in one seasonal cycle, one after the other.
* _**perchlorates**_: These are salts with perchlorate ions that interfere with the thyroid, preventing agriculture on Martian soil.

### Engineering and technology
* _**radioisotope generator**_: A small lump of radioactive material that gives off heat as it decays, serving as a long-lasting power source for Martian rovers.
* _**sorption pumps and beds**_: Devices that capture gas molecules. Bed uses porous material like  molecular sponge, and pump uses 1 or more beds to remove gas from a volume.
* _**gas chromatograph mass spectrometer**_: Heats sample so chemicals become gas and separate, then weighs its molecules to confirm.
* _**Los Alamos lab laser**_: Used on Mars rovers, it fires an intense pulse that vaporises a bit of rock up to 7 metres away, followed by a flash of light to analyse the rock's chemical elements.
* _**breeder reactor**_: A nuclear reactor that creates more fuel than it uses.
* _**Liquid Fluoride Thorium Reactor (LIFTR)**_: A proposed nuclear power plant fed with thorium, which can be bred into uranium-233 as extra fuel.
* _**Phobos Skyhook**_: Proposed space transportation method that takes advantage of Phobos' close orbit to Mars. A tether from Phobos reaches into Mars' skies, allowing Martian rockets to catch it, and be lifted the rest of the way to Phobos or slung to another destination.

These technical topics are fascinating, but I cannot discuss them further as there is nothing I can expand on. Please refer to the book for that information.

## Reasons to occupy Mars

The latter half of the book is less STEM-based, leveraging the author's Earthling social commentary to rationalise how humanity should be on Mars.

The author weaves 3 major reasons for humanity's expansion to Mars. The first is to prevent Western civilisation's collapse. He provides brief historical examples of civilisational decadence leading to destruction, then compares America's current decline into decadence, including symptoms such as people at large being unwilling to think independently or self-reliant, which entrenches impotent bureaucracies and stagnates the economy. The spread of dull popular culture has also eroded Western values of reason, science, and conscience, which in turn makes people irrational perpetuators of the aforementioned problems (p.257-258).

Closely tied to the first, the second reason is to ensure technological progress. The author indicates how slave-based societies do not depend on or aspire toward technological innovation to handle labour shortages. Whereas Western civilisation has aggressively pursued innovation to enslave machines instead, thereby multiplying existing labour productivity (p.263-264). He cites Colonel Robert Ingersoll's 1876 speech, who said that free thought is the root of human progress, creating the machines that removed slavery, made life easier, and endowed wealth (p.189). Expanding to Mars as a new frontier prevents technological stagnation by providing significant change for humanity's further enrichment (p.259).

The third reason is not as deeply discussed, but acknowledges that humanity may be the only life forms to have achieved sentience. This obligates humanity to do what all life does, expand. Though unlike other life, we can consciously do so beyond evolutionary and environmental limitations through terraformation (p.231). Humanity started terraformation via agriculture on Earth, and so it should aim to do the same on other planets. The desire for humanity to achieve as much as it possibly can as a collective species is both pro-human and pro-life. The Mars Society's Founding Declaration states:

_"Alone of the creatures of the Earth, we have the ability to continue the work of creation by bringing life to Mars, and Mars to life"_ (p.282).

## Greatest challenges to Mars occupation

The greatest enemy of humanity as a whole species, is humanity itself. The author specifically identifies groups who would interfere and advocate anti-human philosophies like:

- _**Limited resources and overpopulation on Earth should constrain human aspiration**_ - The author argues that we are not resource limited, otherwise we would always be in stagnation and warring over resources (p.270-272).

- _**It is unethical and imperialist to try to terraform Mars**_ - The author argues that people who believe this are oppressing human rights by treating humanity as a cancer on Earth (p.250).

- _**Prioritise planetary protection of Earth from Martian pathogens**_ - The author argues that bureaucrats do not understand there is no macrofauna or macroflora on Mars to facilitate such pathogens (p.37).

Whilst such anti-life attitudes actively antagonise the pursuit, not discussed in the book is what I feel would be the worst possible enemy, humanity's complacency. The ever-present silent killer of dreams, ambition, and entire civilisations. Complacency's nemesis is curiosity. As long as there is a place where people are free to pursue their scientific curiosity, there is hope.

In my opinion, the second biggest obstacle is Martian gravity (~0.38g) being less than 40% of Earth's. Querying Grok, Mars' gravity would induce bone loss and a weakened heart, because muscle quality retention needs about 70% of Earth's gravity. Unaddressed, it is a hard sell for people to migrate to Mars where they and their offspring will become weaker. The author barely mentions the need for Martians to pursue physical fitness due to this (p.173). What are Martian newborns supposed to do? Won't they be crippled from birth? It is also strange that "gravity" is not even in the book's index, given the criticality of this obstacle for long-term settlement.

Earthling bodies are incompatible with Martian gravity. The pathways to solve this are arguably impractical and certainly controversial. We could change Mars by increasing its mass and therefore gravity to match Earth's, or change Earthlings such that insufficient gravity no longer disrupts the human body. A third option which seems to be the one this book follows by implication, is to send Earthings as-is to Mars accepting the quality of life degradation that those brave and bright souls will face. The thought that it takes humanity's best people to be the pioneer Mars settlers, but have such potentially crushing fates awaiting them, does leave me with mixed feelings.

To be clear, I am not trying to be cynical or pessimistic. I am simply pointing out a problem that absolutely needs to be addressed for the endeavour's long-term success.

## The Martian colonies and their people

Setting that problem aside for now, the people who first move to and settle on Mars would have these attributes:
* Be free-thinking and inventive individualists.
* Are driven for the cause and wanting to achieve significance.
* Openly prepared to cash in all their Earthly possessions.
* Are willing to risk their lives.
* Have the fortitude to overcome seemingly impossible odds.
* Are understanding this is a do-or-die pursuit.
* Seeking the freedom that early colonisers experienced.
  (p.46, 90, 94, 165).

Martian colonies will need to be pragmatic innovator city-states with a smartness, patriotic culture, and civility that protects shared property, whilst surviving by shunning inefficient red tape and disruptive fear-mongering (p.9, 177, 250). The author indicates the most successful city-states will attract women focused on raising families to increase their populations, critiquing that Earth's current population implosion is due to the culture of making women choose between attractive careers and potential single motherhood (p.162-163).

He also critiques how Earth society presently devalues people, like how adolescence is locked away in costly schools and universities, sheltering them from proper work, responsibilities, and integration into adult society. Martian colonies cannot afford such waste, and will need them in more hands-on work experience than school (p.168-170, 186).

## Governance and politics of Martian colonies

The Martian colony will be so intensely driven for survival, that scientific truth, inventiveness, and getting things done would be absolutely essential, and are historically better achieved in more equal societies rather than tyrannical, bureaucratic, or theocratic ones (p.12, 165-166). The author also asserts that pure democracies are dangerous because the people's will is vulnerable to manipulation towards disaster (p.197-198). To combat this, he suggests a common creed among colony members for social solidarity, in place of the Declaration of Independence (p.166, 168).

Beyond this written creed, the author also suggests the cultivation of a general moral patriotism and duty that inspires personal purpose, integrity, self-reliance, responsibility, and team solidarity (p.183). The intention is to raise highly civil and high trust societies, to help keep Martian infrastructure running. I wonder what the author thinks of Singapore as a city-state example. Though the author does discuss how once such infrastructure is in place, people can become privileged, taking it for granted to the point of disrespecting it out of boredom (p.12), or even destroying it.

No country likes to be ruled by another, as history has violently shown. The same applies to planets, in that Martians will pursue governmental independence from Earth (p.195). Continuing the theme of Mars avoiding bureaucracy as essential to survive, the author discusses how laws, tax structure, and the legal system must be simple and widely known so that they do not degenerate into a prosperity-choking protection racket (p.177-179). He also indicates how Mars would have no time for the so-called "warnings" of reactionaries and protectionists (p.223). Such people do not belong in government, as they most likely lack the only guarantee of individual excellence, demonstrated contributions. He argues that such _"...women and men of proven worth, whose wisdom, dedication, and integrity could be counted on to provide the city with the best guidance possible"_ (p.199).

## Martian self-sufficiency

For Mars to be independent, it will need self-sufficiency in its very challenging environment. Still setting aside the gravity problem, sunlight on Mars is only about 40% as that of Earth, with months-long cut-off from dust storms (p.85). Martian soil also impacts the human body, meaning plants need to be grown with energy-intensive artificial illumination from aeroponics or hydroponics instead of in the ground (p.129-130). To power this, the author proposes fusion via Deuterium, which is a plentiful byproduct of Martian water extraction (p.87-88). Given Mars' restriction to greenhouse farming (p.220) and the volume of food people eat in a day, there will be a need to build these greenhouses before people arrive (p.134). The genetic engineering of crops is also necessary for disease immunisation and nitrate production, and of fauna like fish to be grown as larger strains (p.223).

In terms of colony financing, the author argues patents would be the most important Martian export because of their survival-forced innovations in agriculture, recycling, engineering, and automation (p.9). For example, licensing the patents for fission and fusion reactors back to Earth (p.92). I have one misgiving about this, as I believe that the payments from such patents would have greater value on Earth than Mars, given the distance-time differential between the planets. After all, what value is there for a Martian to have Earthling currency, which lacks the liquidity to be immediately traded for goods or services on Mars? It may help fund spaceports on Earth and provisions sent to Mars, and perhaps long-term interplanetary investment, but I am unsure of any other value beyond that.

Despite the necessity of such self-sufficiency efforts, the author also argues that it is undesirable for Mars to be completely self-sufficient (p.84), which is understandable from a political perspective, as having an interplanetary economic relationship curbs the desire for war, by fulfilling desires for profit instead.

## REVIEW

To preface, I was more interested in information technology than the general sciences in school. Perhaps it was due to a lack of interest at the time, something I wish had put more effort into given the hefty maths and physics at the start of this book. It was humourously timed when the author said:

_"If you don't like math, that's okay. Just take my word for it and skip to the next chapter"_ (p.107).

The author made an interesting choice starting with the STEM topics. It meets the expectations of serious readers by giving them claims backed with investigation, and sets tone for the endeavour's complexity. But it also helps turn off quick-to-judge or non-serious readers from buying then badly reviewing the book, as a quick skim throws equations right into their face. A brilliant strategy, if deliberate.

Although I understand that the author may have done so to preserve the optimistic purpose and tone of his book, but skating around the gravity problem places some of his other ideas on unstable foundations. It would have been more intellectually fair to clearly acknowledge the problem and maybe defer its solution to the brave souls who would go to Mars, with Earth help if needed. Or perhaps it is not as grave a problem as I suspect, but an explanation to that effect would have helped.

The physics and maths are reined in from _"Chapter 8: Social Customs on Mars"_, where the book becomes more widely accessible, giving the reader a chance to engage with the author's proposals in non-science areas of Martian civilisation, such as politics and governance being more lightweight and merit-based for efficiency. A few points tend to get repetitive toward the end of the book, like how the author indicates that supporting asteroid mining from Mars would be orders of magnitude easier than from Earth.

A few other interesting things I learned:

* The author admits he played a role in convincing Elon Musk to make Mars settlement his calling, who at the time _"...was looking to do things of immortal importance. Colonizing Mars, solar energy, and electric cars made the cut"_ (p.46).

* There are 2 words for everything in English. Polite upper-class Norman English, and the more direct Saxon English, for example, acquire vs take, dine vs eat, liberty vs freedom (p.187). It reminded me of a few foreign acquiantances who found English difficult. In retrospect, perhaps it was because they unknowingly were actually learning 2 languages at once.

* The 1967 Outer Space Treaty forbids nations from claiming any off-Earth entities like Mars or asteroids as their sovereign territory (p.277). What would happen if there was an asteroid made of solid gold? Unrealistic, but a fun thought.

There are humorous moments but moreso inspiring one-liners throughout the book. Here are three that most resonated with me:

* "Reason proposes, reality disposes" (p.152).

* "...when considering the odds of success in any venture, the quality of resolution is priceless" (p.93).

* _"The game of life is not played for cash. It is played for children, for posterity, for immortality. That is why humans need to go to Mars"_ (p.90).

Though the biggest takeaway I got from this was the effort to reconnect humanity with its core purpose as a lifeform. Humans are a lifeform. The end goal of all life is to expand, with evolution as its means. By that logic, human expansion to the stars, beginning with Mars, is both pro-Life and pro-human.
